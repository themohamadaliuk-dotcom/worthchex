SAVINGS GUIDE

How much emergency savings should I have?

There is no single number that works for every household. A useful emergency fund is large enough to help with essential costs and disruptions, but realistic enough that you can actually build and maintain it.

START WITH ESSENTIAL COSTS

Build the target from the bills you would still have to pay.

Start with expenses that would continue during a disruption: housing, utilities, food, transport, insurance, minimum debt payments and other genuinely essential costs. Optional spending can be considered separately because it may be possible to reduce temporarily.

Stable income

A household with predictable income may choose a different buffer from someone with variable hours, bonuses or self-employed earnings.

Household responsibilities

Dependants, one-income households and other fixed commitments can increase the amount of cash you want readily available.

Known near-term costs

Annual insurance, car repairs, moving costs or planned maintenance may need a separate sinking fund rather than being counted as an emergency.

Example: if essential monthly costs are £1,800, a £5,000 emergency fund covers fewer months than a £10,000 fund. The right target depends on how stable the household income is and what other resources are available.
BUILD IT IN STAGES

A first milestone can be more useful than one giant target.

Waiting until you can save a very large amount can make emergency saving feel impossible. Consider a staged plan: first build a small buffer for a routine surprise, then increase it toward the larger amount you want to protect.

The Savings Goal Calculator can turn the target into a monthly contribution and target date. The Affordability Calculator can also show how a planned purchase would affect the savings buffer you enter.

Keep emergency money accessible

An emergency fund is intended for access when something unexpected happens. Product choice is separate from the size calculation and can depend on access, rate, account rules and your circumstances. MoneyHelper provides general guidance on building savings and choosing savings accounts.

HOW TO SET YOUR TARGET

A simple five-question check

  1. What are your essential monthly costs?
  2. How stable is the household income?
  3. How quickly could you reduce non-essential spending?
  4. What irregular costs are already planned?
  5. How much cash would leave you feeling able to handle an interruption without expensive borrowing?

The answer is personal. The point of the checklist is to create a number based on your actual circumstances rather than copying a fixed rule without context.

FREQUENT QUESTIONS

Emergency-fund FAQs

Should I count my investments as an emergency fund?

That depends on how quickly you can access the money and whether its value could fall when you need it. Keep the emergency-fund calculation focused on resources you would realistically rely on for an urgent cost.

Should an emergency fund include debt payments?

Minimum debt payments are a recurring obligation, so including them can make the target more realistic. The exact amount depends on your household budget.

What if I have a variable income?

A larger cash buffer may be useful because the timing of income can be less predictable. Test a target that reflects the household's actual variability.

How do I balance saving with paying debt?

The right balance depends on the debt's cost, your emergency needs and the risk of being left without cash. A small accessible buffer can sometimes make it easier to avoid new borrowing when a surprise cost appears.

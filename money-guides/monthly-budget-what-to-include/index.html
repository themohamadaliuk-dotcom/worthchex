BUDGET GUIDE

What should I include in a monthly budget?

A useful budget includes recurring costs that genuinely apply to you and makes room for less frequent expenses rather than pretending they do not exist.

THE CORE CHECKLIST

Start with the spending you would have difficulty avoiding.

Home

Rent or mortgage, council tax where applicable, utilities, broadband and other household costs.

Living costs

Food, transport, insurance, childcare, medical or other recurring costs that apply to your household.

Commitments

Minimum debt payments, subscriptions, memberships and other fixed monthly payments.

Then add savings goals and discretionary spending. A budget is more useful when it reflects how you actually live rather than only the bills that appear on statements.

IRREGULAR COSTS

Turn annual bills into monthly planning amounts.

Car servicing, annual insurance, gifts, repairs, school costs and other occasional expenses can make a monthly budget look artificially comfortable if they are ignored. One simple approach is to estimate the yearly amount and divide by 12, then set that amount aside each month.

Example: if a yearly insurance bill is £1,200, setting aside £100 per month turns the annual payment into a known monthly planning cost. When the bill arrives, the money has already been reserved.

The amount does not need to be perfect. The objective is to stop predictable “unexpected” costs from repeatedly breaking the budget.

BUDGET FOR A DECISION

Not every budget needs the same level of detail.

If you are checking whether you can afford a car, focus on the categories that the new commitment could actually affect. If you are planning an emergency fund, focus on essential costs. If you are comparing two jobs, focus on take-home pay and the costs that would change with the move.

FREQUENT QUESTIONS

Monthly budget FAQs

Should savings be treated as an expense?

For planning, it can be useful to show savings as a separate goal rather than treating it as invisible. That makes it easier to see how much room remains after protecting the target.

Should subscriptions be included?

Yes when they are recurring commitments you expect to keep. Small monthly payments can add up and matter when testing a tight affordability scenario.

How should I handle variable spending?

Use a realistic average or a cautious month and then test a higher-spending scenario. A range can be more useful than pretending variable costs are fixed.

Why does my budget look better than my bank account?

Check for annual costs, cash spending, irregular transfers and categories you forgot to include. The quality of an affordability estimate depends on how complete the inputs are.

BUILD A COMPLETE MONTHLY BUDGET

A useful budget includes the costs that do not arrive every month.

Monthly budgeting often looks better when only direct debits are counted. A more realistic plan also converts annual insurance, maintenance, subscriptions paid yearly, school or childcare costs, gifts, travel and other irregular spending into monthly allowances.

Income

Use the income that is actually available for the month being planned. For salary households, compare the figure with a payslip or a take-home-pay estimate.

Fixed and variable costs

Separate unavoidable bills from spending that can move up or down. That makes it easier to test a higher payment or lower income.

Annual costs

Divide predictable annual costs by 12 and set aside that amount each month. This avoids treating the month without a large annual bill as unusually cheap.

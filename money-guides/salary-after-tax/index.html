SALARY GUIDE

How much of your salary do you actually take home?

Your gross salary is the amount before payroll deductions. Your take-home pay is what remains after the deductions that apply to you, which can include Income Tax, National Insurance, pension contributions and student-loan repayments.

2026/27 BASICS

The tax calculation starts with gross pay, but does not end there.

For 2026/27, the standard Personal Allowance is £12,570. In England, Wales and Northern Ireland the main Income Tax rates are 20%, 40% and 45%; Scotland has its own Income Tax bands and rates. Most category A employees pay 8% employee National Insurance between the main thresholds and 2% above the Upper Earnings Limit.

Income Tax

Taxable income is divided across the applicable bands rather than taxed at one percentage from the first pound.

National Insurance

Employee NI uses its own thresholds and categories. It is separate from Income Tax, even though both reduce take-home pay.

Other deductions

Pension contributions and student-loan deductions can reduce the amount that reaches your bank account further.

Official sources: GOV.UK Income Tax rates and allowances · GOV.UK National Insurance · GOV.UK Student Loan tables.

WHY PAYSLIPS DIFFER

The same gross salary can produce different take-home pay.

Two employees can earn exactly the same gross salary and receive different net pay. Tax region, National Insurance category, pension method, pension percentage and student-loan plan can all change the result.

Example: someone earning £40,000 with no pension or student-loan deductions will have a different take-home amount from someone earning the same £40,000 who contributes to a workplace pension and repays a student loan. The gross salary is identical; the deductions are not.

Gross salary versus spending money

Gross salary is useful for comparing job offers. Take-home pay is more useful for household affordability because it represents the money left after the payroll deductions included in the calculation. When planning a new car payment, mortgage, debt payment or savings target, using gross income can overstate the amount you can actually spend.

HOW TO CHECK YOUR OWN NUMBER

Use a calculator, then compare it with your payslip.

Start with the correct annual salary, region and National Insurance category. Add your pension and student-loan choices where relevant. Treat the result as a planning estimate, then compare it with your actual payroll because a tax code, benefits, pay frequency or employer-specific arrangement can change the result.

FREQUENT QUESTIONS

Salary-after-tax FAQs

Why is my take-home pay lower than expected?

Income Tax is only one deduction. National Insurance, pension contributions, student-loan repayments and other payroll deductions can also reduce the amount you receive.

Does Scotland use the same Income Tax bands?

No. Scotland has separate Income Tax bands and rates. National Insurance remains a separate payroll calculation.

Does a pension contribution reduce take-home pay pound for pound?

The effect depends on how the pension is arranged and the tax treatment. The WorthChex calculator models selected common arrangements but is not a full payroll system.

Is take-home pay the same every month?

Not necessarily. Bonuses, overtime, variable hours, benefits, tax-code changes and pay frequency can cause actual payslips to vary.

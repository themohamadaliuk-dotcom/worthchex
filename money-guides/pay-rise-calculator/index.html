SALARY GUIDE

What does a pay rise really mean for your take-home pay?

A 5% or 10% pay rise changes gross salary, but the extra money reaching your bank account depends on Income Tax, National Insurance, pension contributions and student-loan deductions.

GROSS VS NET

A percentage rise applies to gross salary first.

Suppose a salary rises from £40,000 by 5%. The new gross salary is £42,000. The £2,000 gross increase does not normally become £2,000 of extra spending money because additional earnings can create additional payroll deductions.

Gross rise

The percentage is applied to the starting salary to create the new contractual gross amount.

Net rise

The change in take-home pay is what remains after the deductions represented in the calculation.

Monthly difference

For household planning, divide the annual net difference by 12 to get a useful average monthly comparison.

WHY THE NET INCREASE VARIES

The same percentage rise can affect people differently.

Tax region, National Insurance category, pension treatment and student-loan plan can change how much of the increase reaches the employee.

Example: two employees both receive a 5% increase. One has no pension or student-loan deduction; the other contributes to a pension and repays a student loan. Their gross rise is the same, but their estimated net rise can differ.

USE THE EXTRA MONEY CAREFULLY

A pay rise creates more than one budgeting choice.

You may want to increase savings, reduce debt, absorb higher living costs or improve monthly breathing room. The calculator shows the estimated extra take-home pay; the decision about what to do with that amount is separate.

Running a second scenario with a lower pay rise can also be useful when an increase is uncertain or may be staged.

FREQUENT QUESTIONS

Pay-rise FAQs

Does a 5% pay rise mean 5% more take-home pay?

No. The gross salary increases by 5%, but the net increase depends on the deductions that apply to the extra earnings.

Why is my actual increase different from the calculator?

Payroll can include tax-code changes, benefits, pension arrangements, pay frequency and other deductions that a simplified calculator may not model.

Should I budget from gross or net pay?

Use gross pay for comparing employment offers and net pay for household spending decisions.

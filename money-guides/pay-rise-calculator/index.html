PAY GUIDE

Pay rise after tax: how much will you actually get?

You keep less of a pay rise than the headline figure. A £3,000 rise on £30,000 adds about £2,160 a year to take-home pay, or £180 a month, because 20% Income Tax and 8% National Insurance come off the extra.

A pay rise never reduces your take-home pay, because tax is charged in bands: only the money above each threshold is taxed at the higher rate. But a rise that crosses £50,270 or £100,000 is worth noticeably less than one below.

OTHER DEDUCTIONS

Student loans and pensions take a share too.

Plan 2 student loan: on the £30,000 → £33,000 rise, you repay an extra 9%, so you keep about £158 a month instead of £180. That is 63p of each extra pound.

If you pay a percentage of salary into a workplace pension, that grows with your pay too. It is still your money, just saved for later.

REAL TERMS

Is your pay rise beating inflation?

If prices rise 3% and your pay rises 3%, you can buy the same as last year: a real-terms pay rise of zero. To be better off, your take-home pay needs to grow faster than your costs. Compare your percentage rise with the latest CPI inflation figure from the ONS.

FREQUENT QUESTIONS

Pay rise FAQs

Can a pay rise put me in a worse position?

Not through Income Tax or National Insurance alone. It can affect means-tested benefits like Universal Credit, the High Income Child Benefit Charge (from £60,000) and funded childcare (lost above £100,000).

How do I ask for a pay rise?

Gather evidence: what you have achieved, extra responsibilities you have taken on, and what similar jobs pay. Ask for a specific figure.

When will I see it on my payslip?

From the next payroll after the change takes effect. Backdated rises are usually paid as a lump sum, which can be taxed at a higher rate in that one month but evens out over the tax year.

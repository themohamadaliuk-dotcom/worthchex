BUDGET GUIDE

What is disposable income?

Disposable income is the amount left after the regular expenses included in your calculation. It is useful because it shows the monthly room available before you add another financial commitment.

A SIMPLE FORMULA

Start with money available, then subtract the commitments you choose to model.

For a household planning model, take-home income minus included monthly expenses equals disposable income. Common categories are housing, utilities, groceries, transport, subscriptions and debt payments.

Example: if take-home income is £3,000 and the costs you include total £2,200, the modelled disposable income is £800. That does not mean £800 is automatically “free to spend” because annual and unexpected costs may not be in the calculation.
WHAT TO INCLUDE

The quality of the number depends on the quality of the budget.

Regular costs

Housing, utilities, food, transport, insurance, childcare, debt payments and subscriptions that regularly leave your account.

Irregular costs

Annual insurance, repairs, gifts, holidays and other costs that happen less often. Consider a monthly allowance for these if they matter to your decision.

Future commitments

A new car or loan payment should be tested against the disposable income that exists before the commitment is added.

A positive result is not the same thing as a recommendation to spend it all. The calculation is a measure of breathing room under the inputs you have chosen.

WHY IT MATTERS

Disposable income is especially useful for affordability decisions.

Gross salary can look large while take-home pay is smaller. Even take-home pay can overstate available money if important recurring costs are missing. That is why the WorthChex Affordability Calculator asks for actual monthly spending categories and then separates monthly room from savings safety.

FREQUENT QUESTIONS

Disposable-income FAQs

Is disposable income the same as money I can spend?

Not necessarily. It depends on which expenses and irregular costs you included. A careful budget may reserve part of the apparent surplus for annual bills or savings.

Should debt payments be included?

Yes, recurring minimum or contractual debt payments are usually important when assessing household breathing room.

Should I include savings as an expense?

It depends on the question. When assessing a purchase, it can be useful to show the planned savings contribution separately so you can see how much room remains after protecting the goal.

Why is my disposable income negative?

Under the numbers you entered, the included regular costs are greater than the included income. Check whether an input is wrong or whether the budget needs changing before adding another commitment.
